Trading Glossary

From Pip and Spread to Volatility: the most important trading terms — brief, neutral, and sorted alphabetically. Use the search or jump straight to a term via A–Z.

What this glossary is for: It explains common trading terms briefly and neutrally — for educational and informational purposes. B

Bid & Ask #

The bid is the highest price buyers in the market are currently offering — the ask is the lowest price at which sellers will sell. When you buy, you pay the ask; when you sell, you receive the bid. The difference between the two is the spread.

See also: Spread

Breakout #

The moment price leaves a significant zone — such as a resistance, a support, or the boundary of a range. A breakout can be the start of a new move, but it can also turn out to be a false signal (fakeout).

See also: Support & ResistanceRange (Consolidation)Fakeout (Bull/Bear Trap)

Bull & Bear (bullish/bearish) #

The two symbolic animals of the markets: the bull stands for rising prices, the bear for falling ones. Accordingly, a market phase or expectation with an upward tendency is called "bullish", one with a downward tendency "bearish".

See also: LongShortTrend

C

Candlestick Chart #

The most common chart type: each candle summarizes the open, high, low and close of a time period. The body connects open and close, the wicks show the extremes — the color or fill tells you whether the period was rising or falling.

See also: TimeframeGap

CFD (Contract for Difference) #

A contract for difference: you trade only the price difference of an underlying between opening and closing the position, without owning the underlying itself. CFDs are leveraged products — profits and losses are based on the full position size. That is why retail accounts in the EU are subject to leverage limits and negative balance protection.

See also: Underlying (Underlying Asset)LeverageMarginMargin Call

Contract Size #

The standardized quantity of the underlying that a single contract represents — set by the exchange for futures, by the broker for CFDs. Together with the price movement per point, it determines how much money one price step is worth for your position.

→ Go to the position size calculator See also: FutureLotPosition Size

D

Daily Bias #

A directional tendency formulated before the trading day, indicating which way a market is more likely to lean — derived, for example, from higher-timeframe market structure and significant price zones. A bias is a working hypothesis for orientation, not a trading signal and not a prediction.

→ View the current Daily Bias See also: LongShortTrend

Demo Account #

A practice account with virtual funds that lets you try out the platform, order types and workflows without real money. Prices and execution are usually close to reality — but the psychological pressure of real profits and losses can only be replicated to a limited extent.

See also: Market OrderRisk Management

Drawdown #

The decline of an account from a peak to the subsequent low, usually stated as a percentage. If an account falls from €10,000 to €9,000, for example, the drawdown is 10%. The metric describes how much an account has given back along the way.

See also: EquityRisk Management

E

Economic Calendar #

A schedule of upcoming economic data and events — such as interest rate decisions, inflation figures, or US labor market data — including the time and an assessment of market relevance. Around high-relevance releases, volatility, spreads, and slippage risk often increase significantly.

→ This week's events in the calendar See also: VolatilitySpreadSlippage

Equity #

The current total value of a trading account: account balance plus unrealized profits or minus unrealized losses of open positions. As long as positions are open, equity fluctuates with every price update.

See also: DrawdownMargin

F

Fakeout (Bull/Bear Trap) #

An apparent breakout after which price quickly falls back into the previous zone. If a breakout to the upside fails, it is called a bull trap; if one to the downside fails, a bear trap — in both cases, traders who bet on the continuation get trapped.

See also: BreakoutSupport & Resistance

FOMO (Fear of Missing Out) #

The fear of missing a price move — it tempts traders to jump into moves already underway without a plan, often near a local high or low. FOMO is one of the best-known psychological pitfalls in trading and encourages overtrading.

See also: OvertradingRisk ManagementBreakout

Future #

A standardized, exchange-traded forward contract with a fixed contract size and a fixed expiry date. Unlike CFDs, futures trade on a futures exchange (e.g. CME or Eurex); before expiry, an open position is usually rolled into the next contract.

See also: Underlying (Underlying Asset)Contract SizeCFD (Contract for Difference)

G

Gap #

A price jump with no trading in between: the first price after a trading break is significantly above or below the last one before it — leaving a gap on the chart. Gaps occur mainly over the weekend or after news outside trading hours; stop orders can then be filled at considerably worse prices than planned.

→ View market trading hours See also: SlippageVolatilityStop-Loss

L

Leverage #

Allows you to control a larger position with little capital: with leverage of 1:30, your stake controls 30 times its value in position size. Profits and losses are based on the full position size — so leverage magnifies both and increases the risk accordingly.

See also: MarginMargin CallCFD (Contract for Difference)Risk Management

Limit Order #

An order that is only executed at the specified price or better. You control the execution price this way, but there is no guarantee that the market will reach your limit and that the order will be filled at all.

See also: Market OrderStop OrderSlippage

Liquidity #

Describes how easily a market absorbs buying and selling without the price jumping sharply. High liquidity usually comes with tight spreads and stable order execution; in thin market phases, the risk of slippage increases.

See also: SpreadSlippageVolume

Long #

A position that bets on rising prices: you buy first and sell later — ideally at a higher price. The opposite is short.

See also: ShortBull & Bear (bullish/bearish)

Lot #

A standardized trading quantity in forex: a standard lot equals 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot 10,000, a micro lot 1,000. Together with the pip value, the lot size determines how much a price move changes your account.

See also: Pip ValuePosition SizeContract Size

M

Margin #

The collateral your broker blocks on the account when you open a leveraged position. If the free margin no longer suffices because of running losses, the broker can forcibly close positions (margin call or stop-out).

See also: LeverageEquityMargin Call

Margin Call #

The broker's notification that your account's equity is no longer sufficient to cover the required margin of the open positions. If equity keeps falling, the broker closes positions automatically (margin close-out) — for retail accounts in the EU, at the latest when it drops below 50% of the required margin. There is no obligation for retail accounts in the EU to pay in more than the deposited capital — negative balance protection applies.

See also: MarginLeverageEquity

Market Order #

An order that is executed immediately at the next available price. Execution is therefore very likely, but the exact price is not guaranteed — in fast markets it can deviate from the displayed quote due to slippage.

See also: Limit OrderStop OrderSlippage

O

OCO Order (One Cancels the Other) #

Two linked orders where the execution of one automatically cancels the other. A typical example: the stop-loss and take-profit of the same position — if price reaches one of the two levels, the other order is removed.

See also: Stop-LossTake-ProfitLimit Order

Overtrading #

Taking significantly more trades than your own rulebook allows — often triggered by boredom, frustration, or the urge to win losses back immediately. Since every additional trade brings costs (spread, possibly slippage) and new risk, overtrading is considered one of the most common mistakes in trading.

See also: FOMO (Fear of Missing Out)Risk ManagementSpread

P

Pip #

The smallest standardized price movement of a currency pair — the fourth decimal place (0.0001) for most pairs, the second (0.01) for yen pairs. If EUR/USD rises from 1.1000 to 1.1001, the price has moved one pip.

See also: Pip ValueSpread

Pip Value #

The amount of money one pip of price movement is worth at your position size. It depends on the currency pair, lot size, and account currency — and thereby translates stop distances from pips into a concrete amount of money.

→ Use it in the position size calculator See also: PipLotPosition Size

Position Size #

The number of units, lots, or contracts traded in a single trade. It can be chosen so that a triggered stop-loss only costs the pre-planned share of the account — determined from account size, risk per trade, and stop distance.

→ Calculate your position size See also: Risk ManagementStop-LossLot

R

Range (Consolidation) #

A market phase in which price moves sideways between a recognizable upper and lower boundary instead of forming a trend. Ranges often occur when supply and demand temporarily balance out — for example, in anticipation of important news.

See also: VolatilityBreakoutDaily Bias

Risk Management #

The umbrella term for all rules that keep losses controlled and plannable: fixed risk per trade, stop-loss, appropriate position size, and daily or weekly limits. The goal is not to avoid losses, but to prevent a single trade from seriously damaging the account.

→ Calculate your risk per trade See also: Position SizeStop-LossDrawdownRisk-Reward Ratio (RRR)

Risk-Reward Ratio (RRR) #

Puts the potential profit of a trade in relation to the risk defined in advance. A ratio of 2:1 means: the distance from entry to take-profit is twice as large as the distance to the stop-loss. The ratio only describes this relationship — it says nothing about the probability of an outcome.

See also: Stop-LossTake-ProfitRisk Management

S

Short #

A position that bets on falling prices: you sell first (borrowed or replicated via a derivative) and buy back later — ideally at a lower price. The opposite is long.

See also: LongBull & Bear (bullish/bearish)

Slippage #

The difference between the expected and the actually executed order price. Slippage occurs mainly in fast or thin markets — for example around important economic news — and can work for or against you.

→ High-impact news in the calendar See also: SpreadMarket OrderLiquidity

Spread #

The difference between the buy price (ask) and the sell price (bid) of an instrument — it is an immediate cost incurred every time you open a position. In liquid market phases the spread is usually tight; at night or around news it can widen considerably.

See also: Bid & AskLiquiditySlippage

Stop Order #

An order that only becomes active once price reaches a specified level — and is then usually executed as a market order. As a stop-loss it closes an existing position; as a buy stop or sell stop it opens a new one as soon as price reaches the level.

See also: Stop-LossLimit OrderMarket Order

Stop-Loss #

An order that automatically closes your position when price reaches a predefined level against you. It limits a trade's loss to the planned amount — although in very fast markets, execution can deviate from the stop price due to slippage.

See also: Take-ProfitTrailing StopStop OrderPosition Size

Support & Resistance #

Price zones where the market has repeatedly turned in the past: support levels lie below the current price, resistance levels above it. Such zones serve as reference points in technical analysis — but there is no guarantee that price will react there again.

See also: BreakoutFakeout (Bull/Bear Trap)Range (Consolidation)

Swap (Financing Costs) #

Costs or credits incurred when you hold a leveraged position overnight — also called rollover or overnight financing. The amount depends, among other things, on interest rate differentials and the broker's conditions; to cover the weekend, three times the rate is often charged on a fixed weekday.

See also: CFD (Contract for Difference)LeverageMargin

T

Take-Profit #

An order that automatically closes your position as soon as price reaches a predefined profit target. Together with the stop-loss, this fixes the risk-reward ratio of a trade before you even enter it.

See also: Stop-LossRisk-Reward Ratio (RRR)OCO Order (One Cancels the Other)

Timeframe #

The period of time a single candle on the chart summarizes — for example one minute (M1), 15 minutes (M15), one hour (H1), or one day (D1). The same market can look very different depending on the timeframe: higher timeframes show the big picture, lower ones the movement in detail.

See also: Candlestick ChartTrendTrading Session

Trading Session #

The trading phases of the major financial centers — Sydney, Tokyo, London, New York — that divide the day into windows of varying activity. Activity in the currency markets is usually highest during the London and New York sessions and their overlap.

→ Trading hours at a glance See also: LiquidityVolatility

Trailing Stop #

A stop-loss that follows price at a fixed distance as long as the trade moves in your favor — it never moves against you. This lets you lock in part of the open profit without setting a fixed profit target.

See also: Stop-LossTake-Profit

Trend #

A market phase in which price predominantly moves in one direction: an uptrend forms higher highs and higher lows, a downtrend lower ones. If price moves sideways without a clear direction, it is called a range.

See also: Range (Consolidation)Support & ResistanceDaily Bias

U

Underlying (Underlying Asset) #

The financial instrument a derivative is based on — for example an index, a commodity, a stock, or a currency pair. CFDs and futures track the price movement of their underlying without you having to own it yourself.

See also: CFD (Contract for Difference)Future

V

Volatility #

The extent of a market's price fluctuations within a period of time. High volatility means larger moves — and thus greater opportunities and risks alike; around important economic news it often spikes suddenly.

→ View the economic calendar See also: LiquidityRange (Consolidation)

Volume #

The quantity of an instrument traded within a period of time, for example in shares, contracts, or lots. Volume shows how active a market currently is and often serves as an indication of how broadly a price move is supported.

See also: LiquidityVolatility
